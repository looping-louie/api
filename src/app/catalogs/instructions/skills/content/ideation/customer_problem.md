# Customer Problem

## Scope

This skill covers the analysis and expression of a customer or user problem
before implementation planning.

It applies when a proposal needs clarity about:

- who experiences the problem;
- what they are trying to achieve;
- when and where the problem occurs;
- how frequently it occurs;
- how serious its consequences are;
- how people solve it today;
- what evidence supports the problem;
- whether different users experience different versions of it.

The purpose is to prevent a team from treating an assumed solution request as a
validated problem.

## Problem framing

### Identify the affected customer

Describe the narrowest useful customer or user group.

Distinguish where relevant:

- buyer;
- end user;
- administrator;
- operator;
- approver;
- beneficiary;
- affected third party.

Do not use broad labels such as "businesses," "users," or "teams" when the
workflow depends on a more specific role, context, or level of expertise.

A useful customer definition includes the conditions under which the problem is
experienced.

### Identify the customer job

Describe what the customer is trying to accomplish.

The job should remain meaningful even if the proposed product does not exist.

Examples of job dimensions include:

- making a decision;
- completing a transaction;
- coordinating people;
- reducing uncertainty;
- meeting a deadline;
- complying with a requirement;
- recovering from a failure;
- demonstrating progress;
- finding or comparing information.

Avoid defining the job using the proposed feature.

### Describe the current situation

Explain:

- the trigger that starts the workflow;
- the steps the customer currently takes;
- tools, people, or workarounds involved;
- where delays, errors, uncertainty, or frustration appear;
- what happens when the customer does nothing;
- which constraints shape the current behaviour.

The current alternative may be:

- a competitor;
- a spreadsheet;
- email;
- manual work;
- an internal team;
- postponement;
- accepting the problem.

"No solution" is still a competing alternative.

### Separate symptoms from causes

A symptom is an observable negative effect.

A cause explains why the effect occurs.

For example:

- symptom: reports are delivered late;
- possible cause: source data arrives through incompatible manual processes.

Do not commit to one cause unless evidence supports it.

When several causes are plausible, preserve them as hypotheses.

### Assess severity and frequency

Evaluate:

- how often the problem occurs;
- how many people or processes it affects;
- time lost;
- money lost or delayed;
- risk introduced;
- emotional or organisational friction;
- quality degradation;
- missed opportunities;
- regulatory or contractual exposure.

A frequent minor inconvenience and a rare catastrophic failure require
different product responses.

### Identify evidence

Separate evidence from belief.

Possible evidence includes:

- direct customer statements;
- observed workflows;
- support requests;
- product analytics;
- failed transactions;
- sales objections;
- churn reasons;
- operational records;
- search behaviour;
- financial impact;
- compliance findings;
- repeated manual work.

Record the strength and limitations of the evidence.

Avoid treating stakeholder confidence as customer evidence.

## Problem statement construction

A useful problem statement should answer:

- Who experiences the problem?
- What are they trying to achieve?
- In what context does it happen?
- What prevents or slows progress?
- What do they do today?
- What negative outcome results?
- What evidence indicates this matters?

Keep the problem statement independent from the preferred solution.

### Desired outcome

Express the outcome as a change in customer capability, behaviour, quality,
speed, confidence, or cost.

Avoid outcomes that merely restate delivery, such as:

- launch the feature;
- create the page;
- add automation.

Prefer outcomes such as:

- customers can compare options without manual research;
- operators can detect failed jobs before customers report them;
- teams can produce an approved implementation plan in one session.

## Segmentation checks

Different customer groups may have different:

- goals;
- urgency;
- purchasing authority;
- workflows;
- constraints;
- risk tolerance;
- technical ability;
- regulatory obligations.

Do not merge segments when doing so creates contradictory requirements.

When one segment should be prioritised, state why.

## Validation checks

Check that proposed validation:

- tests whether the problem exists;
- reaches the actual affected customer;
- observes behaviour rather than only stated preference;
- explores current alternatives;
- tests severity and urgency;
- can reveal that the problem is not worth solving;
- avoids leading questions tied to the proposed solution.

Useful discovery questions include:

- Tell me about the last time this happened.
- What triggered the situation?
- What did you do next?
- Which part was hardest or slowest?
- What did the problem cost?
- Who else became involved?
- What happens when it is not solved?
- Which alternatives have you tried?

## Common failure patterns

### Customer defined too broadly

Correction:

- identify the role, context, and trigger;
- separate buyer and user where needed.

### Feature request treated as a problem

Correction:

- ask what outcome the requested feature should produce;
- document the existing workflow and pain.

### Unsupported root cause

Correction:

- distinguish observed symptoms from causal hypotheses;
- add validation for each important hypothesis.

### Weak urgency

Correction:

- assess frequency, severity, cost, and consequences of inaction.

### Ignoring current alternatives

Correction:

- document manual processes, competitors, postponement, and internal work.

### Leading validation

Correction:

- investigate recent real behaviour before presenting a solution.

## Output qualities

A strong customer problem definition is:

- specific;
- evidence-aware;
- solution-independent;
- connected to a real workflow;
- explicit about severity and frequency;
- clear about current alternatives;
- suitable for shaping and prioritisation.

It should help determine whether a proposed idea addresses an important problem
for a sufficiently specific customer.
